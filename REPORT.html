Sixteen identical options.
One of them gets 83%.

Ask Jev to choose between options it has no reason to prefer, and it does not shrug. It has a favourite — and the favourite is decided by what you happened to name things.

Model jev‑1.13.0 Option sets tested 273 Pre‑registered before data 19 September 2026

The question

What breaks the tie?

A Jev choice question hands the model a list of options and asks it to pick one. Most of the time the content decides. But plenty of real questions are close calls, and something has to break the tie.

We wanted to know what that something is. So we removed the content entirely. Every question in this experiment says the same thing: here are sixteen options, nothing favours any of them, pick one.

With sixteen interchangeable options and no information, a well-behaved model should spread itself evenly — 6.25% each, one in sixteen. Anything else is a preference the model brought with it. And since we stripped out everything else, the only thing left that could carry a preference is the names of the options.

Finding one

There is a favourite, and it depends on the names

We ran five sets of thirty questions, each using a different style of option name. If names were irrelevant, every bar below would sit on the dashed line. None of them do, and the spread between them is enormous.

Finding two

It is not alphabetical order. It is the letter a.

The obvious explanation is that the model sorts your list and likes whatever lands on top. That explanation is wrong, and one comparison kills it.

Every list has an option that comes first alphabetically. Sometimes that option starts with a — amber, anchor. Sometimes the list has no a‑word and the winner starts with something later — kettle, silver. Both are equally first. If sorting were the mechanism, both should get the same boost.

This is the whole result in one picture. A sorting rule is relative: whoever happens to be earliest in this particular list wins. A preference for the letter a is absolute: only names that actually start with a win. The data says absolute.

Finding three

Nothing is being sorted at all

There is a second, cleaner test. If a computer were sorting your option list, names beginning with a digit would land dead first — that is simply how sorting works in code. So digit‑led names should win most often.

They win least often. By a wide margin.

Finding four · the one that will bite you

Numbered options are a trap

The letter a is worth about double. Numbers are worth far more, because two separate effects stack on top of each other.

83%
of the entire distribution goes to item1 when the options are item1 through item16 and the question is a close call. Its fair share is 6.25%.

Why two effects, not one

Not all our numbered lists started at 1 — some ran from 2, some from 3. Splitting them apart separates the two things going on.

The same shape turns up in random IDs. Among UUID options, the ones beginning with 0 take 2.39× their fair share and the ones beginning with 1 take 1.22×; everything else sits at or below fair. Jev is reaching for whatever token looks like the start of a familiar sequence — the letter a, the digits 0 and 1, the lowest number in a run.

A correction

Why earlier work reported something different

An earlier exploratory pass on this model reported that the alphabetically‑first option takes 80% of the mass — far more than the modest effect we found on ordinary words. That finding rested on three option sets.

It was not wrong. We sent those exact three sets again and reproduced the numbers almost precisely (0.85, 0.73, 0.82 against the reported 0.83, 0.73, 0.80). The problem was what they were made of: names like aaa, bbb and tag_a, tag_b — names that differ only by one letter of the alphabet.

Does it matter in practice?

We flipped a real decision

Everything above uses questions with no content, which is the worst case by design. So we tried a genuine one: a support ticket where a customer was charged after cancelling and cannot sign in. Billing or technical? A real judgement call.

We asked the same question four times, rotating which category sat in which key slot. A system reading the content gives the same answer all four times.

What to do about it

Five rules

Never number your options.queue1, tier1, item1 is the single worst pattern, because two effects stack there.
Give every option a description.This is what moves the decision from the label to the content. It was the difference between a stable answer and a flipping one.
Don't let one key lead with a, 0 or 1.Unless you actually want that option favoured on close calls.
If your keys must be opaque, naming can't save you.For document IDs or retrieval candidates, rotate the candidates across two calls and average, or move the identifier into the description.
Test it on your own traffic.Take a borderline case and rotate your options through the key slots. If the verdict moves, your naming is doing the deciding. It costs two API calls.

How it was run

273 option sets in total: 150 for the main experiment (five naming styles, thirty questions each, sixteen options apiece) and 123 for a follow‑up that tested why earlier results differed. Every hypothesis and every statistical test was written down and committed before any data was collected.

Jev returns identical numbers for identical requests, so repeating a question tells you nothing. The thing being counted is distinct option sets, not API calls — which is why thirty of them is the meaningful number, not thirty repeats of one.

The statistics

Exact binomial tests against a one‑in‑sixteen null, Clopper‑Pearson intervals, and Holm correction across six hypotheses. Thirty sets per group gives better than 99% power against the effect sizes reported here.

letter a vs later: p = 2.3e‑5 name style: p = 3.0e‑8 digit 1 bonus: p < 1e‑12 numeric vs lexicographic: p < 1e‑12

What this doesn't show

  • One model version on one day. The sizes belong to that snapshot.
  • Most measurements use questions with no content, which is the worst case on purpose. Where options carry real meaning, meaning usually wins.
  • The a‑and‑1 explanation came from looking at the data after the fact. It fits everything we measured, but it deserves its own pre‑registered run before anyone calls it settled.
  • We tested one ticket in the practical section, not a traffic sample. Rule five is how you find out what it costs you.
